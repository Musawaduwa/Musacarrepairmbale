🔋 Why Does My Car Battery Drain Overnight?

You park the car at night and next morning the battery is flat. If the battery is healthy, something is drawing power while the car is off. This is called a parasitic drain.

Common causes

  • A door, boot or glove-box light that stays on because of a faulty switch.
  • Badly fitted accessories: alarms, trackers, amplifiers, dashcams and USB chargers.
  • A relay stuck closed, or a control module that never goes to sleep.
  • A faulty alternator diode that leaks current back through the alternator.
  • Damaged or water-affected wiring.
  • An old battery that cannot hold a charge.

First quick checks

  1. Make sure all lights are off and every door and the boot fully close.
  2. Unplug phone chargers and aftermarket devices.
  3. Look for lights that stay on inside the car after locking.
  4. Have the battery load-tested. A weak battery can look like a drain.

How a mechanic finds the drain

With the car locked and all modules asleep (wait 20 to 40 minutes), a multimeter set to amps is connected in series with the battery. A normal healthy car draws only a few tens of milliamps. A reading much higher means a circuit is awake. The mechanic then pulls fuses one at a time until the current drops, which shows which circuit has the fault, and tests parts on that circuit.

⚠️ Safety: Do not disconnect the battery while the engine is running, and avoid sparks near the battery. Be careful on cars with airbag or radio codes; ask a professional first.

Prevention

Have accessories fitted by a competent auto electrician, keep terminals clean (see our battery terminal guide), and replace batteries older than 3 to 4 years when they weaken.

🔌 Alternator Not Charging: Signs, Tests and Causes

The alternator charges the battery and powers the car while the engine runs. When it fails the car runs on battery power alone, then stops.

Warning signs

  • Battery warning light on the dashboard.
  • Dim or flickering headlights, especially at idle.
  • Whining or growling noise from the engine bay.
  • Slow windows, weak wipers, or radio cutting out.
  • Car starts after a jump but dies again soon.
  • Smell of hot wires or burning rubber.

Simple voltage test

  1. Set a multimeter to DC volts (20 V range).
  2. With the engine off, a healthy battery shows about 12.4 to 12.7 V.
  3. Start the engine. The reading at the battery should rise to roughly 13.5 to 14.7 V.
  4. Switch on headlights and the heater fan. The voltage should stay above about 13 V.

If voltage stays near battery voltage, or goes above about 15 V, the charging system needs attention.

Common causes

  • Worn or loose drive belt (see our drive belt guide).
  • Worn brushes or bearings inside the alternator.
  • Failed rectifier (diode) or voltage regulator.
  • Corroded or loose battery terminals, or a bad earth strap.
  • Broken or burnt wiring to the alternator.
⚠️ Safety: Do not disconnect the battery to test the alternator on modern cars; it can damage electronics. Keep hands away from the belt and fan when the engine runs.

Alternator problems and bad connections look alike, so test first before buying a new part.

💡 Headlights Dim, Flickering or Not Working: Causes and Fixes

Weak lights at night are dangerous. The cause is usually simple, but you must find the right one.

Only one headlight is out

Most often it is a blown bulb. Check the bulb filament, and the connector for melting or corrosion. If the new bulb fails again, look for a poor earth or a damaged plug.

Both headlights are out

Check the headlight fuse and relay, the light switch and the wiring. Both going out together points to something shared, not the bulbs.

Lights are dim

  • Old or darkened bulbs, or cloudy and yellow lenses.
  • Corroded connectors and poor earth connections that add resistance.
  • Weak battery or failing alternator (lights get brighter when you rev the engine).
  • Very thin or damaged wiring, or cheap high-watt bulbs overloading the circuit.

Lights flicker

Loose connectors, a failing alternator, a damaged wire that moves with bumps, or a faulty switch. Flickering that follows road bumps usually means a loose connection.

Basic checks

  1. Test the bulbs and try swapping left and right.
  2. Check fuse and relay.
  3. Look for burnt or green connectors.
  4. Clean and tighten the earth point.
  5. Check charging voltage (see alternator guide).
⚠️ Safety: Do not touch halogen bulbs with bare fingers, let bulbs cool before changing them, and never use bulbs of higher wattage than the car is designed for. HID/xenon lights use very high voltage; leave them to a professional.

Good aftermarket upgrades need a relay harness. Wiring a headlight upgrade directly through the original wires can overheat the circuit.

🧾 OBD2 Trouble Codes Explained: P0300, P0171, P0420 and More

When the check engine light comes on, the car stores a code that a scan tool can read. The code points to a circuit or system, but it does not always tell you which part is bad.

How to read a code

The first letter shows the system: P powertrain (engine and gearbox), B body, C chassis, U network. For P codes, a first digit 0 means a generic code shared by all makes. The last digits identify the fault.

Common codes

  • P0300: random or multiple cylinder misfire. Check plugs, coils, fuel and air leaks.
  • P0301 to P0308: misfire in a specific cylinder (P0302 is cylinder 2). Swap the coil or plug to see if the fault follows.
  • P0171 / P0174: system too lean. Often an air leak, dirty MAF sensor or weak fuel pressure.
  • P0172 / P0175: system too rich. Leaking injectors or faulty sensors.
  • P0420: catalyst efficiency below threshold. Could be a worn catalytic converter, but also an exhaust leak or a faulty oxygen sensor.
  • P0335 / P0340: crankshaft or camshaft position sensor circuit. The engine may crank but not start.
  • P0113 / P0118: intake air or coolant temperature sensor circuit fault.

Why not just replace the part?

Many drivers replace the part named by the code and the light returns. A code like P0420 can come from a wiring or exhaust leak rather than the converter. Good diagnosis checks live data, wiring, connectors and the actual cause.

Driving an older Toyota or Nissan? See our Toyota and Nissan fault codes, manual diagnosis and pinouts guide.

After repair

Codes are cleared after the repair is done and the car is test-driven. Clearing codes alone just hides the problem; it will come back.

⚠️ Note: A flashing check engine light means a serious misfire. Avoid heavy acceleration and get the car checked soon.

📟 How to Use a Multimeter on a Car: Beginner Guide

A multimeter is the most important tool for car wiring. With three simple tests you can find many faults.

1. Measure voltage (DC V)

Set the dial to DC volts. Red probe to the battery positive (+), black probe to negative (−). A rested healthy battery reads about 12.4 to 12.7 V. Below about 12.0 V the battery is discharged. With the engine running you should see roughly 13.5 to 14.7 V.

2. Check continuity (beep mode)

Disconnect the battery, then touch the probes to each end of a wire or fuse. A beep or near-zero reading means the path is complete. No beep means a break. Use this to test fuses, switches and wires.

3. Find a voltage drop

A bad connection can have 12 V on one side but not deliver current. With the circuit working, measure voltage across a connector or switch. More than about 0.3 to 0.5 V across it shows a poor connection or corrosion.

4. Test an earth (ground)

Measure from the component's earth point to the battery negative while the circuit is on. A reading above a few tenths of a volt means a bad earth.

Good habits

  • Always select the right mode before touching probes.
  • Measure resistance and continuity only with power off.
  • Never measure current by connecting probes across the battery; that creates a short circuit.
  • Use sharp probes carefully and never pierce wire insulation unless you seal it afterwards.
  • Do not test airbag circuits with a multimeter.
⚠️ Safety: Remove rings and watches, avoid sparks near the battery, and disconnect the negative terminal before working on wiring.

A multimeter shows what is happening in the circuit. For hidden or intermittent faults, bring the car to a workshop with fault-tracing experience.

🧠 ECU Problems, Fault Codes and Pinouts (Toyota and Nissan)

The ECU (engine control unit) is the car's engine computer. It reads sensors and controls fuel injection, ignition and more. Because it is so central, drivers often blame it, but most "ECU problems" are actually caused by wiring or sensors.

Signs the ECU may be faulty

  • Engine cranks but does not start, with no spark or no injector pulse.
  • Random stalling or misfires on all cylinders.
  • Check engine light that will not clear, or no communication with the scan tool.
  • Fuel pump or cooling fan running at wrong times.
  • Immobiliser or security light problems.
  • Damaged connector, water inside the ECU, or a burnt smell.

What damages an ECU

  • Water entering the engine bay or cabin (floods, washing the engine).
  • Reversed battery polarity or incorrect jump starting.
  • Short circuits in wiring or a failing injector that pulls too much current.
  • Poor earthing or voltage spikes from a faulty alternator.
  • Heat and age, or poor-quality repairs.

Test before you replace

  1. Check battery voltage and earth connections.
  2. Check ECU fuses, relays and power and ground wires to the ECU.
  3. Check main sensors, especially crank and cam position sensors.
  4. Inspect connectors for water or green corrosion.
  5. Scan for communication errors.

Repair or replace?

Many ECUs can be repaired by replacing failed components such as driver transistors, capacitors or damaged tracks, which is often cheaper than a new unit. A replacement or second-hand unit may need coding to match the car's immobiliser and keys. Always find and fix the cause first; otherwise the new ECU can fail again.

⚠️ Safety: Never open or probe an ECU on the car with the battery connected. Disconnect the battery first and avoid static electricity.

Before you start: how fault codes work

A fault code (DTC) is a message stored by the ECU when a sensor, circuit or part is outside its normal range. The code says where to look, not always which part has failed. Wiring, connectors, earths and low battery voltage cause many codes.

  • Blink (flash) codes are read with a jumper wire and the warning light. They are used on older Toyota and Nissan cars (roughly 1980s to late 1990s).
  • OBD2 codes (P0xxx, P1xxx) are read with a scan tool on the 16-pin socket under the dashboard. They are used on most cars from about 1996 onwards (earlier in some markets).
  • Many Japanese imports in Uganda have both: a blink-code system for older models and OBD2 for newer ones.
ℹ️ Note: No website can list every code for every model, because there are thousands and many differ by engine and year. Below are the standard and most common ones. Always confirm with a scan tool or the workshop manual for your exact car.
⚠️ Safety: Switch off the ignition before connecting or removing any jumper. Never short any terminal you are not sure about. Never probe or jump airbag circuits except as the airbag section below describes.

Toyota: read codes manually

A. Older engine check connector (under the bonnet)

Look for a small rectangular box, often with a flip lid, near the fuse box, firewall or strut tower. Inside are labelled terminals such as TE1, TE2, E1, T, TC, W, TS.

  1. Battery voltage should be above 11 V. Warm the engine to normal temperature, then switch the engine off.
  2. Switch the ignition ON (engine not running).
  3. Use a jumper wire (paper clip or insulated wire) to connect TE1 and E1 (on some models the terminal is marked T).
  4. Watch the check engine lamp on the dash (or the lamp inside the ECU on some models). Lamp steady-blinking every 0.25 second means no codes stored.
  5. If there are codes, the lamp blinks the first digit, pauses, then blinks the second digit. A longer pause comes before the next code. After all codes, a pause of about 4 to 5 seconds, then the sequence repeats.
  6. Example: 2 blinks, pause, 2 blinks = code 22 (coolant temperature sensor).
  7. Remove the jumper and switch off the ignition when done.

B. OBD2 socket on 1996+ models (DLC3)

On many Toyotas the same test can be done at the 16-pin socket by jumping pin 13 (TC) to pin 4 (CG, chassis ground), with the ignition on. Pins 5 (SG), 7 (SIL), 6 and 14 (CAN H and L) and 16 (battery positive) are the other important pins. Newer cars (about 2005 onwards) use CAN and normally need a scan tool instead.

C. Clearing codes

After fixing the fault, switch off the ignition and remove the EFI fuse (or the +B / ECU-B fuse) for at least 10 seconds, or disconnect the battery negative for several minutes. Colder weather needs longer. Re-read the codes. If the same code returns, the fault is not fixed.

ℹ️ Note: Newer ECUs may need two drive cycles with the same fault before a code is stored, so a code may take a little time to appear.

Nissan: read codes manually (ECCS ECU mode selector)

Most Nissans from the mid-1980s to the mid-1990s with ECCS (for example Sunny, Pulsar, Skyline, Pathfinder, Maxima) have a diagnostic ECU with one or two LEDs (red and green) and a small mode selector screw on the side or top.

Find the ECU

Common places: under the passenger side kick panel, behind the glove box, or under the centre console. Remove the trim and open the ECU cover.

Steps

  1. Ignition ON, engine off.
  2. Turn the diagnostic mode selector fully clockwise and wait. The LEDs will flash together and a number of flashes shows the mode (1 to 5). Fully turning again after a pause changes to the next mode on some ECUs; use the manual for your model.
  3. For stored fault codes use Mode 3 (Stored Fault Code reporting).
  4. Read the LEDs: red flashes = first digit, green flashes = second digit. One red and two green = code 12; five red and five green = code 55.
  5. After reading, turn the selector fully counter-clockwise and switch off the ignition.

Other modes

ModeUse
Mode IOxygen sensor monitor: shows whether the O2 sensor is switching.
Mode IIMixture ratio control monitor: shows if mixture is rich or lean.
Mode IIIStored fault codes (most used).
Mode IVSwitch on/off monitor: checks key start, idle and vehicle speed switches.
Mode VReal-time diagnosis: you can tap wiring or a connector and the ECU shows a fault if a connection is intermittent.

Clearing codes

On many models, switch the selector to the diagnostics position and then back to normal, then switch off the ignition. On others, disconnect the battery negative for several minutes. Re-test.

Newer Nissans (about 1995 onwards)

These often use four-digit codes (for example 0505 means no fault stored) and need a CONSULT unit or a generic OBD2 scanner. On OBD2 cars use the 16-pin socket.

Pin diagnosis: testing an ECU connector step by step

Pin diagnosis means measuring voltages and signals at the ECU connector, pin by pin, to find whether the ECU, wiring or a sensor is at fault. It is the best way to avoid replacing a good ECU.

⚠️ Safety: Switch off ignition before connecting or disconnecting the ECU. Never insert probes into the front of a connector; use back-probing from the wire side or a break-out box so pins are not bent. Do not use a bulb test light on sensor circuits. Never apply 12 V to a sensor or signal pin, because it can destroy the ECU.

Tools

  • Digital multimeter (with Hz and DC volts)
  • Fine back-probe pins or break-out harness
  • Wiring diagram or terminal list for the car
  • Injector test light (noid light) and spark tester
  • Oscilloscope (best for crank, cam and injector signals)
  • Jumper wires and a battery charger

Procedure

  1. Check the battery first. Charge it above 12.4 V. Many codes and no-starts come from low voltage.
  2. Identify the connector and pin. Look at the connector face (not the wire side) for the numbers. Use the car's wiring diagram. Never assume wire colour.
  3. Power and grounds. Test +B, BATT, IGSW and every earth pin (E1, E2, E01, E02). A missing power or a poor earth is the most common ECU "fault".
  4. Reference voltage. Test VC (about 5 V). If VC is missing or low, a shorted sensor may be pulling it down. Unplug sensors one at a time and see if it recovers.
  5. Sensor signals. Compare each signal to the table below at the correct temperature.
  6. Output signals. While cranking, check injectors, ignition and fuel pump relay control.
  7. Wiring continuity. With the ECU and sensor unplugged and the battery disconnected, test each wire for continuity from sensor to ECU, and for shorts to earth or to other wires.
  8. Wiggle test. Gently move the harness and connectors while watching the reading to find intermittent faults. On Nissan Mode V does the same job.
  9. Decide. If power, grounds, reference, sensors and wiring are all good and outputs are still missing, only then is the ECU suspect.

🌡️ Engine Overheating: Causes, Coolant Care and What to Do

An overheating engine can destroy itself in minutes. Act at the first sign.

What to do

  1. Switch off the air conditioner and turn the heater to full hot to move heat away from the engine.
  2. Pull over safely and switch off the engine.
  3. Wait at least 30 minutes for it to cool.
  4. Check the coolant level in the reservoir and top up with coolant or clean water.
  5. If it overheats again, do not drive. Call a mechanic.
⚠️ Safety: Never open a hot radiator cap. Hot steam and coolant can cause serious burns.

Common causes

  • Low coolant or a leak.
  • Failed thermostat or water pump.
  • Broken drive belt or cooling fan.
  • Blocked radiator.
  • Head gasket failure.

Check your coolant

Clean coolant is bright and clear (green, pink or blue depending on type). Brown, oily or rusty coolant needs flushing.

Change coolant about every two years, or as the manual says, and fix leaks early.

💨 Exhaust Smoke Colours and What They Mean

Smoke from the exhaust is a clue. Its colour points to the likely fault.

Black smoke

Too much fuel is burning (rich mixture). Common causes are a dirty air filter, leaking injectors or faulty sensors. You may notice poor fuel economy.

Blue smoke

Engine oil is burning, often from worn piston rings or valve seals. You will use more oil than normal. Check the oil level often.

White smoke

Thick white smoke that does not clear, with a sweet smell, can mean coolant is entering the engine, often from a damaged head gasket. Check for falling coolant level and milky oil. Thin white vapour on a cold morning is normal condensation.

Grey smoke

Often linked to a clogged PCV valve, but it can also come from automatic gearbox oil being sucked in or other faults. Have it checked.

⚠️ Safety: If smoke comes with overheating, loss of power or warning lights, stop driving and get a diagnosis.

⚙️ How to Check Your Car's Drive Belt

The drive belt turns the alternator, power steering pump, water pump and A/C compressor. If it breaks, the battery stops charging and the engine can overheat quickly.

Signs it needs replacing

  • Squealing or chirping noise, especially at start-up or in rain.
  • Cracks across the ribs, fraying edges or missing chunks.
  • Shiny, glazed or hardened surface.
  • Battery light, overheating or heavy steering.

How to check

  1. Switch off the engine and let it cool.
  2. Look at the belt with a torch, bending it slightly to open any cracks.
  3. Check the tension: it should not feel loose or slack.
  4. Check pulleys for wobble or noise.
⚠️ Safety: Keep hands, hair and clothes away from the belt when the engine is running. If the belt breaks, stop the car immediately.

Replace belts according to your manual (often between 60,000 and 100,000 km) or earlier if worn.

🕹️ Automatic Gear Selector Explained: P, R, N, D, L, 2, 3

An automatic car has a few simple positions. Using them correctly protects your gearbox and keeps you safe.

What each position does

  • P (Park): locks the transmission when parked.
  • R (Reverse): moves the car backward.
  • N (Neutral): no power to the wheels.
  • D (Drive): normal forward driving with automatic shifts.
  • L (Low): more power and engine braking for steep hills.
  • 2 / 3: limit the highest gear, useful on slopes.

Good habits

  1. Press the brake pedal before moving the selector out of P.
  2. Come to a complete stop before shifting between D and R.
  3. On slopes, apply the handbrake first, then shift to P.
  4. Use L or 2 on long downhill roads to save the brakes.
⚠️ Never shift into P or R while moving, and do not coast downhill in N.

🔋 How to Clean Battery Terminals (Even With Coca-Cola)

White, green or blue powder on the battery clamps blocks electricity. It causes slow starting, flickering lights and "no start" problems, and cleaning it is one of the cheapest repairs you can do.

Why it forms

Gas and moisture from the battery react with the metal clamps. Loose clamps, an old battery or a leaking battery make it worse.

What you need

  • Spanner (usually 10 mm or 12 mm)
  • Old toothbrush or wire brush
  • Baking soda and water, or a little Coca-Cola
  • Clean cloth, plain water, grease or Vaseline
  • Gloves and eye protection

Steps

  1. Switch off the engine and remove the key.
  2. Loosen and remove the negative (black, −) clamp first, then the positive (red, +).
  3. Pour a little Coca-Cola over the residue, or use a paste of baking soda and water. It will fizz.
  4. Scrub the clamps and battery posts with the brush until you see clean metal.
  5. Rinse with a small amount of clean water and dry completely.
  6. Reconnect the positive first, then the negative, and tighten firmly.
  7. Smear a thin layer of grease or Vaseline to slow new corrosion.
⚠️ Safety: Battery acid burns skin, eyes and clothes. Never let a spanner touch both terminals, and keep flames away from the battery. Cola works because it is mildly acidic, but it leaves sugar behind, so always rinse and dry. Baking soda is the better choice.

When cleaning isn't enough

If the battery is swollen, leaking, more than 3 to 4 years old, or the car still struggles to start, have it tested.

🛞 Brake Dust and Worn Brakes: What Your Wheels Are Telling You

Some dark dust on your wheels is normal because brake pads wear slowly as they work. The warning signs are how much dust, where it is, and how the car feels.

What the dust can tell you

  • One wheel much dirtier than the others: a sticking brake caliper or a dragging brake on that wheel.
  • Very heavy dust and fast wear: low-quality or very soft pads, or heavy braking on hills.
  • Shiny metal-like dust or scratches: pads are worn out and metal is cutting the disc.

Signs of bad brakes

  • Squealing noise when braking (wear indicator).
  • Grinding sound: pads are finished and discs are being damaged.
  • Car pulls to one side.
  • Steering wheel or pedal vibrates (warped discs).
  • Soft or sinking pedal (air or leak in the brake fluid system).
  • Brake warning light on.

Quick check

Look through the wheel spokes at the pad. If it is thinner than about 3 mm, replace it soon. Check the disc for deep grooves or a rusty, lipped edge.

⚠️ Safety: Brake dust is harmful to breathe. Do not blow it off with your mouth or compressed air. Wash it off with water and a cloth. Never ignore grinding or a soft pedal; get the car checked immediately.

Care tips

Replace pads in time, change brake fluid every two years, avoid riding the brakes downhill, and have brakes checked at every service.

🛢️ How to Check Engine Oil Level and Condition

Engine oil keeps the engine cool and lubricated. Low or dirty oil is one of the biggest causes of engine failure, and checking takes two minutes.

How to check the level

  1. Park on flat ground and switch off the engine.
  2. Wait 5 to 10 minutes so the oil drains back into the sump.
  3. Pull out the dipstick (usually yellow or orange handle) and wipe it clean.
  4. Push it fully back in, then pull it out again.
  5. The oil should be between the MIN and MAX marks. Top up slowly if it is low, using the correct grade from your manual.

What the oil condition means

  • Golden or light brown: clean and fresh.
  • Dark brown or black: due for an oil change, especially if thick.
  • Milky, creamy or frothy: coolant is mixing with the oil, which can mean a head gasket problem. Stop driving and see a mechanic.
  • Gritty or shiny metal particles: internal engine wear.
  • Smells of petrol: fuel is leaking into the oil.
  • Very low or empty: top up before driving and look for leaks.
⚠️ Safety: The engine and oil can be very hot. Do not overfill; too much oil is also harmful. If the red oil-pressure light comes on, stop the engine immediately.

How often

Check at least once a month and before long trips. Change oil and filter about every 5,000 km, or sooner on dusty roads.

📑 In this guide

  2. Fuse box abbreviations and meanings
  3. How to find, check and replace a fuse

Where to find the fuse box

Most cars have two: one inside the cabin (under the dashboard, near the driver's knees or behind a side panel) and one under the bonnet near the battery. The lid or the owner's manual has a diagram showing which fuse controls what.

How to check and replace a fuse

  1. Switch off the engine and remove the key.
  2. Find the fuse for the faulty item using the diagram.
  3. Pull it out with the small puller clip (or thin pliers).

How power flows in a Toyota fuse box

Most Toyotas use a "chain" so each switched circuit has the right protection:

  1. Battery to ALT or MAIN (heavy fusible link or maxi fuse) into the fuse box.
  2. AM1 and AM2 supply the ignition switch. When you turn the key, they feed IG1 and IG2 (ignition-controlled power).
  3. ACC is powered in accessory and on positions (radio, lighter).
  4. ST or STA powers the starter signal only while cranking.
  5. ECU-B is always live (memory and wake-up). ECU-IG is live only with the key on.
  6. Individual fuses then protect each circuit: stop lights, wipers, fuel injection, and so on.

If many unrelated things stop working together, check the common upstream fuse (for example AM1, AM2, IG1, ALT or MAIN) before blaming the parts.

🚨 Dashboard Warning Lights Explained

Dashboard lights are your car's way of reporting a problem. The colour tells you how urgent it is.

Colour guide

  • 🔴 Red: serious. Stop safely as soon as you can.
  • 🟠 Amber/Yellow: a fault or service needed soon.
  • 🟢 Green/Blue: a system is on (lights, indicators, cruise).

Important lights and what to do

  • Oil pressure (oil can): stop the engine immediately and check oil level. Do not drive.
  • Battery: charging fault (alternator, belt or battery). Drive carefully to a workshop; the car may die soon.
  • Engine temperature / coolant: overheating. Stop, switch off and let the engine cool before opening anything.
  • Brake warning: low brake fluid, handbrake on or brake fault. Check the handbrake first; if still on, do not drive.
  • Check engine: steady means a fault is stored (get a scan). Flashing means a serious misfire; reduce speed and stop driving soon.
  • ABS: normal brakes work, but anti-lock does not. Get it checked.
  • Airbag: the airbag system may not work in a crash.
  • Tyre pressure (TPMS): a tyre is low. Check and inflate.
  • Seatbelt: someone is not buckled up.
⚠️ Safety: Do not ignore red lights or a flashing check-engine light. Switching the light off or removing a bulb does not fix the problem.

When lights stay on after starting

All lights turn on briefly when you switch on the ignition, then go out. Any light that stays on means the car has stored a fault, and a scan tool will read the cause.

🔑 What to Do If Your Car Won't Start

Photo: Michael Mroczek, CC0 (public domain) via Wikimedia Commons.

A car that will not start usually gives a clue. Listen to what happens when you turn the key.

1. Nothing happens, no lights

The battery is probably flat or a terminal is loose. Check the battery clamps are tight and not covered in white or green powder. Clean and tighten them.

2. Fast clicking sound

The battery is too weak to turn the engine. Try a jump start (see the links below) or have the battery tested.

3. Engine turns but won't start

The battery is fine, so look at fuel and spark. Is there fuel in the tank? Does the fuel pump hum when you switch on? Faulty coils, sensors or a blocked filter are common causes.

4. Single loud click, then nothing

This often points to a bad starter motor or poor connection to the starter.

5. Starts, then dies

Could be a dirty idle valve, air leak, fuel pump problem or crank sensor fault.

⚠️ Safety: Never smoke or use a flame near the engine. Do not keep cranking for more than 10 seconds at a time because it can overheat the starter. If you smell fuel, stop and call a mechanic.

Quick checklist

  1. Gear in Park/Neutral and brake pressed.
  2. Check battery terminals.
  3. Check fuel level.
  4. Try a jump start with an experienced helper.
  5. If it still fails, do not keep trying; get it diagnosed.

🛞 Flat Tyre or Burst Tyre: What to Do

A burst tyre is frightening but manageable if you stay calm.

If a tyre bursts while driving

  1. Hold the steering wheel firmly with both hands.
  2. Do not slam the brakes.
  3. Ease off the accelerator and let the car slow down.
  4. Brake gently once the car is under control.
  5. Switch on hazard lights and pull off the road onto firm, flat ground.
⚠️ Safety: Never change a tyre close to moving traffic or on a slope or soft ground. Put a warning triangle or leafy branch behind the car, and keep passengers away from the road.

Changing a flat tyre

  1. Park on flat, hard ground, apply the handbrake and put the car in gear or Park.
  2. Loosen the wheel nuts a half turn while the wheel is on the ground.
  3. Place the jack at the jacking point shown in the owner's manual and lift the car.
  4. Remove the nuts and the wheel, fit the spare and hand-tighten the nuts.
  5. Lower the car, then tighten the nuts in a star pattern.
  6. Check the spare's pressure and drive slowly to a tyre shop.

Prevent bursts

Check tyre pressure every two weeks, replace tyres that are cracked, bulging or worn smooth, and do not overload the car. Rough Ugandan roads and hot weather increase the risk.

🛑 Brake Failure: What to Do and How to Prevent It

Complete brake failure is rare, but knowing what to do can save lives.

If the brakes fail while driving

  1. Stay calm and warn others with hazard lights and the horn.
  2. Pump the brake pedal quickly several times; this can rebuild pressure.
  3. Shift down to a lower gear to use engine braking (manual: change down step by step; automatic: select a low gear).
  4. Apply the handbrake slowly and steadily, not suddenly, so the car does not skid.
  5. Steer toward a safe place such as an open grass or sandy area, or an uphill road to slow down.
  6. As a last resort, rub the side of the car lightly along a barrier or bank to scrub speed.
⚠️ After stopping: Do not drive again. Have the car towed to a workshop.

Warning signs

  • Soft or spongy pedal that sinks.
  • Grinding or squealing noises.
  • Car pulls to one side when braking.
  • Brake warning light on.
  • Vibration in the pedal or steering.
  • Burning smell after driving downhill.

Prevention

Check brake fluid level, replace worn pads before they grind the discs, change brake fluid every two years, and fix leaks immediately. On long downhill roads, use a lower gear instead of riding the brakes.

⚡ 7 Signs Your Car Has an Electrical Problem

Modern cars depend on wiring, sensors and computers. When something fails, the symptoms are often confusing. Here are seven common signs that point to an electrical fault.

1. Car is slow to start or clicks

A weak battery is the usual cause, but a loose terminal, worn starter or poor earth connection can give the same symptom. Check the terminals for white or green corrosion first.

2. Lights flicker or dim

Flickering headlights or dashboard lights often mean a failing alternator or a loose connection. If lights dim at idle and brighten when you rev the engine, have the charging system tested.

3. Repeated blown fuses

A fuse blows to protect a circuit. If the same fuse keeps blowing, replacing it with a bigger one is dangerous; the real cause is a short circuit or damaged wire that must be traced.

4. Warning lights on the dashboard

Battery, check-engine, ABS and airbag lights usually mean a sensor or wiring fault is stored in the car's computer. A diagnostic scan reads the code and shows where to look.

5. Burning smell or hot wires

This is serious. Stop driving, switch off and disconnect the battery if safe. Overheated wiring can cause a fire.

6. Power windows, locks or radio work intermittently

Often caused by worn wiring in door hinges or a poor earth. These faults come and go, which makes experienced fault tracing important.

7. Engine stalls or misfires

Faulty coils, crank or cam sensors and damaged harnesses can all cause misfires and stalling.

🔥 Engine Misfire: Causes, Symptoms and Fixes

A misfire happens when one or more cylinders fail to burn fuel properly. The engine shakes, loses power and the check-engine light may flash.

Common symptoms

Rough idle, jerking when accelerating, poor fuel economy, a petrol smell from the exhaust and a flashing check-engine light.

Main causes

Ignition: worn spark plugs, weak ignition coils or damaged plug leads. Fuel: blocked or leaking injectors, weak fuel pump or dirty fuel. Air: vacuum leaks or a dirty throttle body. Mechanical: low compression from worn rings, valves or a head gasket. Electrical: faulty crankshaft or camshaft sensors and wiring faults.

How diagnosis works

A scanner shows which cylinder misfires (for example P0302 is cylinder 2). The mechanic then swaps or tests the coil and plug, checks injectors, measures compression and inspects the wiring to that cylinder.

Is it safe to drive?

A flashing check-engine light means unburnt fuel is reaching the catalytic converter and can damage it. Drive only a short distance, gently, to a workshop.

Prevention

Replace spark plugs on schedule, use clean fuel, change air filters and fix warning lights early.

🧰 Car Maintenance Guide for Ugandan Roads

Dust, potholes, heat and heavy rain make maintenance more important in Uganda. Use this checklist to keep your car reliable.

Every week

Check tyre pressure, engine oil level, coolant and battery terminals. Make sure all lights work.

Every 5,000 km

Change engine oil and the oil filter. On dusty roads, inspect the air filter and clean or replace it earlier than the book says.

Every 10,000-20,000 km

Check brake pads and discs, rotate tyres, inspect suspension bushes and ball joints (rough roads wear them quickly), and test the battery and alternator.

Every 40,000 km

Replace spark plugs, fuel filter and gearbox oil as the manufacturer recommends, and flush the coolant.

After rainy season or flooding

Check wiring, connectors and earths for moisture and corrosion. Water in connectors is a common cause of sensor faults and warning lights.

Warning signs not to ignore

Grinding brakes, steering pulling to one side, overheating, oil or fuel smell and new warning lights.
